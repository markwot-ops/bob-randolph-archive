Bob Randolph

El Tecolote Blanco · the White Owl · Revolutionary Poet

About Bob

Bob Randolph wrote for decades under his own name and under the pen name El Tecolote Blanco — "the White Owl." He was a UC Berkeley–trained economist whose California state career ended after he invoked the Fifth Amendment before the House Un-American Activities Committee over a 1960s trip to Cuba; blacklisted from his profession afterward, he supported himself through teaching, newspaper writing, and freelance real estate appraisal — while writing poetry that never stopped being political.

He was named House Poet of the Radical Philosophy Association in 1999, and his poems were read at RPA's national conferences and its Cuba Conferences through the 2000s. He read regularly at Berkeley's Fellowship Café & Open Mic and the Berkeley Senior Center, and was billed by Poetry Flash in 2004 as "Cuban poet Bob Randolph, author of Cuban Poems."

Reading the World

Bob took his poems well beyond Berkeley. He had two radio shows in the Bay Area and gave readings in Los Angeles and New York. In 1994 he read at the largest English-language bookstore in Paris, and in 1998 he toured England. By 1999 his poems had appeared in eleven publications, and he had written some 1,700 of them. That August, Nigel Summerley of the Times of London, writing about Berkeley since the 1960s, met Bob on Shattuck Avenue and called him “a prolific and perceptive poet.” Summerley wrote that Bob performed for him on the sidewalk and had him crying with laughter. Thomas Sanders, who wrote the introduction to Bob's chapbook, circulated the clipping that November with a letter calling Bob “a modern Isaiah.”

The Crucible of North Berkeley

Bob's politics ran like a live wire straight through the radical Bay Area of the 1960s and '70s. While others merely participated in the counterculture, Bob observed it with the sharp, discerning eye of a journalist. Covering the story as an older journalist for the New York radical newspaper the National Guardian, he filed dispatches from the front lines of a changing America. He was there on October 1, 1964, when the Free Speech Movement exploded into history. Bob chronicled the pivotal, 32-hour standoff on Sproul Plaza where thousands of students surrounded an immobilized police car to prevent an arrest. He watched, notebook in hand, as a young orator named Mario Savio removed his shoes to avoid damaging the vehicle, climbed onto the roof of the trapped police car, and delivered the fiery, impassioned speeches that made front-page news across the globe. Through Bob's reporting, the image of Savio holding forth atop an automobile became an indelible symbol of the era's generational revolt.

Poetry and the Panther Era

For all his radical positioning, Bob was never a dogmatist. He had a journalist's healthy skepticism and a poet's intolerance for performative chaos. He explored the clandestine anarchist meetings that sprang up in Berkeley's backrooms and basements, and did not hold the movement in high regard; to Bob, its ideology lacked the structured discipline that real liberation required. His allegiance leaned toward the organized resistance of the era's revolutionaries. Bob became a familiar, trusted figure around the Black Panther Party and Eldridge Cleaver. He dedicated his chapbook El Tecolote Blanco: Space and Time Travel to Cleaver, who was known to have slept on Bob's couch, and Cleaver wrote the introduction to another, El Tecolote Blanco: A Bob Randolph Sampler (1995), in which he calls Bob the “new Poet Laureate of the U.S.A.”

The Last of the Bay Area Beats

Bob published the way the Beats did, in chapbooks, the small stapled booklets that movement revived in postwar San Francisco to put uncensored writing straight into readers' hands. In his later years he became the living center of the North Berkeley poetry scene. While San Francisco's North Beach was the home of nocturnal jazz clubs, North Berkeley, especially the “Gourmet Ghetto” along Shattuck Avenue, was an open-air salon where academics, anti-war activists and the literary underground mixed freely. Even into his nineties, Bob held court daily at Peet's Coffee and Tea in North Berkeley. He didn't argue or anger in person, only in his poetry; in person he was a gentle, bigger-than-life figure, always in good humor, who loved life. He was among the last living links to a generation of Bay Area poets who took their work to the streets — to be read aloud. And his work never wavered from the conviction: that poetry and political struggle are not separate arts, but of the very same breath.

Generosity

Bob was generous in practice, not only in verse. At 88, when he sold the building he lived in, in rent-controlled Berkeley, he made it a condition that the current tenants could stay as long as they wished. He gave money to people on the street and to just about anyone who asked, and when he could he gave work instead of a handout, hiring street people for jobs of his own. He bought art from a neighbor who lived in senior housing, Larry Melnick, a world-class painter, so that Larry could have a one-man show chronicling a lifetime of work, just months before Larry died of pancreatic cancer. Bob read his poems at the show, and some of those readings are in the Taped Live Readings. Larry's artwork is on the cover of Bob's self-published 1991 book, I AM WRITING OF HAND GRENADES, BUTTERFLIES AND KISSES. Bob consciously supported both Larry and Harold Murphree in the last months of their lives. Harold died unexpectedly, and his story is part of the book told on the Books page.
