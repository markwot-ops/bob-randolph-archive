The Battle of Hastings

The Norman horsemen rushed at the Saxon foot soldiers, toward the edge of the meadow, sabers or cross bows drawn, peering through shield helmets. Aron of the Saxons, blond, sidestepped and lunged at the charging Friant, a muscled Norman dark-haired one, dear to William himself. The horse whinny-screamed as Aron's dagger struck his rib cage. Horse saliva was smeared on Aron's shoulder. Friant whirled and struck with his saber at two Saxons, Aron and another, but the blade whistled above their heads. Again Friant charged and as the horse reined up at the men, he was pitched to the ground, still gripping the handle of his saber. The rising roar and screech of the battle became deafening. Men and horses whirled charged roared screamed whinnied grunted clashed, metal fell heavily. Mon Dieu, Mien Gude, Mein Gott. Friant swung his long saber. Aron ducked the blade struck another Norman on the chest who fell heavily under another Norman horse. Aron pulled a dagger from his tunic and lunged at Friant, still the closest Norman, who was struggling to regain his balance, Aron's blade catching Friant's gloved hand, ripping the saber from his grasp. The Frenchman jerked his own dirk from his mailed loincloth and stood facing Aron, arms held wide. Both were in battle-poise, daggers drawn. Aron dropped his dagger and pulled his short-handled axe from his belt behind. All England was in the balance, arrows flew, sabers clashed, men lay groaning in blood on every side. Aron and Friant lunged and stabbed, whirled and gripped eath other, seizing the other's weapon wrist. The power of Norman France, Normandy, England, Hastings. Aron and Friant, arms wrestled trembling overhead, empires in blood and sabers, courage fear death and hideous wounds, spurting blood from men and horses. Friant jerked suddenly free and plunged his dirk into Aron's middle, who brought his short axe suddenly down in his recoiling agony and it plunged into Friant's neck, a severed ear hanging swinging, as blood spurted. and Aron's own torn abdomen, his clothing now warm and sticky with blood. Empire with terrible fear. Oh mon dieu. Oh, God. I'm dying, finish me off, they crawled toward each other. Horses careening close by, they reached to finish the kill, their fingers clawed at the other's face. Their bodies moved so little now. Both lay, close by, almost as though in rest after some dread and unforgettable ecstasy. The battle of Hastings, was fought the rest of that day, October 14, 1066, with screams and steel, blood and brains, terror and triumph, empire and the silence of defeat that lasts until both the winners and losers merge after a thousand years into the same society and language, with no one living who knew or honored those other Saxons or Normans on that other day so long ago, or ever knew the names of Aron, the Saxon, or Friant, the Norman . . .
