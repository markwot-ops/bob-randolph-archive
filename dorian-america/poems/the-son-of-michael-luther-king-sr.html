The son of Michael Luther King, Sr.

There was nothing unusual about Martin. He was born of a Baptist preacher's wife in Atlanta in 1928, January 15 to be exact. He was born after difficult labor, a round and quiet little soul that stared at you trying to focus glazed eyes. He was like any other middle class kid in Atlanta, except that he was black, a "Negro" they said back in those days, with some Irish blood in him too. But that didn't make any difference when later he played baseball and football on the corner lot. He was a tough competitive kid, everybody knew that. Everybody picked him first when they chose up sides. I suppose they also played "war" once in a while, and there'd be a big to-do over which side would be the "Germans". It was the Great Depression, even bigger than the present one, the biggest failure of capitalism ever. Oh, sure, World War I was a failure of capitalism, but everyone worked. You didn't see hundreds standing in line in dark overcoats in the cold. But Martin's family did okay, only two generations from share-cropping. His dad pulled himself up and became a preacher and at least that gave you some kind of a living. His dad took a rundown debt-ridden Baptist church, and pumped life into it, with the sheer force of his will, and even built seven choirs in that one church. A church choir, the sound of a church choir, one of the greatest human sounds on earth. People flocked into these warming nests of emotional rhythms, these clusters of singing Teddy Bears, smiling wide, feeling fulfillment, a sanctuary from the prejudice in the streets. Martin's childhood was favored, there was money during the Depression, his father was influential, and Martin had a chance to see the power of the spoken word, how congregations could be ignited and moved to action by words shared by all of them, spoken by one person. He began to want to be such a figure, to speak for the Lord, to speak for his people. He once said to his mother, "You just wait and see. I'm going to get me some big words." And so he went through childhood, and in a display of baffling complexity, the future great Martin Luther King, Jr., attempted suicide not once but twice before he was 13 years of age. Both occasions involved his beloved grandmother, Jennie Williams, first when she was knocked unconscious by Alfred Daniel, his little brother, when that grandmother died in 1941. As Lerone Bennett, Jr., one of King's biographers says of Martin's grandfather: Part Negro and part Irish, James Albert King, the beginning, was a hard-working sharecropper who dreamed of buying a small farm for his family. But as he sank deeper and deeper into debt, he gave up hope and spent his weekends to drink himself into forgetfulness. James Albert King's soil, a soil that yielded peasants, paupers, murderers, robbers and finally Luther King, was that soil of sorrow. To work from sun-up to sun-down for a whole year and to end owing 'the man' $400 for the privilege of working to do this year after year and to sink deeper and deeper into debt; to be chained to the land by bills at the plantation store; to wash away this knowledge with bad gin, to blot it out in an ecstasy of song and prayer; to sing, to pray, to cry... The James Kings bore 10 children, one of which was Martin's father - Michael Luther King, born in 1899. His name did not become Martin Luther King until he was 22 years old - Martin, the name his father James wanted him to have, a name to become known world-wide a generation later. All the usual things happened to Martin, Jr., at adolescence exclusion from play with his white friends, at school, bright and precocious, and skipped the 9th grade, his father, growing in power in the black community received his share of threats from the Ku Klux Klan in Atlanta. Martin Jr. had his first cigarette behind a fence, a smooth talker with girls with whom he danced at every chance, and changed girlfriends like he changed the tweed suits he adored. He would "go to the grass" with peers, to settle differences by physical combat, winner take all. He was a typical, tough, bright, middle class kid, who struggled like others with being a male and being a black male. And so . . . , we know about Rosa Parks in 1955 and Montgomery, and we know about Birmingham and Selma and Atlanta and the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church and the Ebenezer Baptist Church, and Martin Luther King's ties with Ghandi, and later the Nobel Peace Prize and that ghastly motel balcony . . . in Memphis in 1968 and his incredible leadership and statesmanship in the years in between - and we also can value these words his wife Coretta chose of the hundreds of thousands he spoke, that left their mark on our understanding of the nature of power and privilege in the world that all lay on the unique foundation he learned from Ghandi - the concept of power, human power, for all of us of every race, the power based on resistance, loving resistance, but at all costs resistance, to stand tall and quiet and define yourself by your own knowledge and love for others, and your own recognition that millions love you too. Here are some of the words of her husband Martin that Coretta King chose among all the things he said in his life: he once said - As long as there is poverty in the world I can never be rich, even if I have a billion dollars. As long as diseases are rampant and millions cannot expect to live more than 30 years, I can never be totally healthy. I can never be what I ought to be until you are what you ought to be. No individual or nation can stand out boasting of being independent. We are interdependent. New laws are not enough. The emergency we now face is economic, and it is a desperate and worsening situation. For the 35 million poor people in America, there is a kind of strangulation in the air. In our society it is murder, psychologically, to deprive a man of a job or an income. You are in substance saying to that man that he has no right to exist. He said to us, when evil men plot, good men must plan. when evil men burn and bomb, good men must build and bind. when evil men shout ugly words of hatred, good men must commit themselves to the glories of love. When millions have been cheated for centuries, restitution is a costly process. Inferior education, poor housing, unemployment, inadequate health care - each is a bitter component of the oppression that has been our heritage. Each will require billions to correct. Justice so long deferred has accumulated interest and its cost for this society will be substantial. And again, the deep rumblings of discontent that we hear today is the thunder of disinherited masses, rising from dungeons of oppression to the bright hills of freedom, in one majestic chorus the rising masses singing, i n the words of our freedom song, 'Ain't gonna let nobody turn us around.' All over the world, like a fever, the freedom movement is spreading in the widest liberation in history. He reminded us, the Bible tells the thrilling story of how Moses stood in Pharaoh's court centuries ago and cried, 'Let my people go.' A true revolution of values will lay hands on the world order and say of war: "This business of burning human beings with napalm, of filling our nation's homes with orphans and widows, of injecting poisonous drugs of hate into the veins of peoples normally humane, of sending men home from dark and bloody battlefields physically handicapped and psychologically deranged, cannot be reconciled with wisdom, justice, and love. A nation that continues year after year to spend more money on military defense that on programs of social uplift is approaching spiritual death. He taught us, the greatest irony and tragedy of all is that our nation, which initiated so much of the revolutionary spirit of the modern world, is now cast in the mold of being an arch antirevolutionary. We are engaged in a war - the war in Vietnam - that seeks to turn the clock of history back and perpetuate white colonialism. He cried out, "we cannot remain silent as our nation engages in one of history's most cruel and senseless wars. During these days of human travail we must encourage creative dissenters. We need them because the thunder of their fearless voices will be the only sound stronger than the blasts of bombs and the clamor of war hysteria.." He sang to us, Hatred and bitterness can never cure the disease of fear; only love can do that. Hatred paralyzes life; love releases it. Hatred confuses life; love harmonizes it. Hatred darkens life; love illumines it. He said, something should remind us once more that the great things in this universe are things that we never see. You walk out at night and look up at the beautiful stars as they bedeck the heavens like singing lanterns of eternity and you think you can see all. Oh, no You can never see the law of gravitation that holds them there. Love is the most durable power in the world. This creative force, so beautifully exemplified in the life of our Christ, is the most potent instrument available in mankind's quest for peace and security. He admonishes us, the belief that God will do everything for man is as untenable as the belief that man can do everything for himself. It, too, is based on a lack of faith. We must learn that to expect God to do everything while we do nothing is not faith but superstition. Forgiveness is not an occasional act, it is a permanent attitude, he reminds us. And so, this is the giant, god-like stature of Martin Luther King, Jr., who became known world-wide and is revered everywhere as a good and brave man; who once smoked his first cigarette behind a fence, like many of us, flirted with the girls liked others his age, wondered about the meaning of life, struggled with and chose his path in the church, then found himself unexpectedly challenged and invited to live a life of greatness, one that will echo down through the next two or three centuries as one of the most precious and bravest lives ever lived on this earth.
