Chapbooks

The Last Chapman

A chapbook is a small, inexpensive booklet—usually under fifty pages, saddle-stitched or stapled at the spine, and bound tightly around a single theme or a connected run of poems. The name stretches back to the “chapman,” the old English word for the itinerant peddlers who carried woodcut-illustrated booklets in their packs, selling them door-to-door for pennies. Popular across Europe from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries, these pocket-sized prints democratized literacy, carrying everything from bawdy ballads and folklore to radical religious tracts.

In the mid-twentieth century, this low-fidelity medium was revived by the American Beat poets. In the smoky, postwar air of San Francisco, the chapbook became the ultimate weapon against mainstream publishing houses—a way to get uncensored, counterculture writing into readers' hands quickly, cheaply, and with the ink practically still wet. This raw, DIY lineage is exactly how Bob distributed his own work. He published the way the Beats did, operating in that same fertile slipstream of North Beach and Berkeley, a world fueled by self-published mimeo-machine zines and politically charged verse.

A Voice Forged in Jazz and Verse

Bob’s writing style was defined by its relentless, rhythmic urgency. His poems did not sit quietly on the page; they were written to be read aloud, inheriting the jazz-infused cadence of early performance poetry and the booming power of public rallies. He stripped away academic pretense in favor of a raw, colloquial voice—pairing cosmic imagery of space and time travel with the gritty, concrete realities of street-level resistance. By publishing his collection, El Tecolote Blanco: A Bob Randolph Sampler, through his own independent Randolph House Press in Berkeley, Bob ensured his typography—haphazardly aligned and stamped onto cheap paper—carried the same rebellious energy as the words themselves.

The Crucible of North Berkeley

Bob’s politics ran like a live wire straight through the radical Bay Area of the 1960s and ’70s. While others merely participated in the counterculture, Bob observed it with the sharp, discerning eye of a journalist. Operating as a West Coast correspondent for the British newspaper The Guardian, he filed dispatches from the front lines of a changing America, translating the seismic shifts of Berkeley to an international audience. He was there on October 1, 1964, when the Free Speech Movement exploded into history. Bob chronicled the pivotal, twenty-two-hour standoff on Sproul Plaza where thousands of students surrounded an immobilized police car to prevent an arrest. He watched, notebook in hand, as a young orator named Mario Savio removed his shoes to avoid damaging the vehicle, climbed onto the roof of the trapped police car, and delivered the fiery, impassioned speeches that made front-page news across the globe. Through Bob's reporting, the image of Savio holding forth atop an automobile became an indelible symbol of the era's generational revolt.

Poetry and the Panther Era

Yet, for all his radical positioning, Bob was never a dogmatist. He possessed a journalist's healthy skepticism and a poet's intolerance for performative chaos. He frequently explored the clandestine anarchist meetings popping up in Berkeley’s backrooms and basements—a movement he ultimately did not hold in high regard. To Bob, their ideology lacked the structured discipline required for true liberation. He viewed their chaotic rhetoric as a distraction from the real, coordinated labor of social change. Instead, his allegiance leaned toward the organized resistance of the era's true revolutionaries. Bob became a familiar, trusted figure around the Black Panther Party, eventually dedicating his chapbook El Tecolote Blanco: Space and Time Travel to Panther leader Eldridge Cleaver who was known to have slept on Bob’s couch.

The Last of the Bay Area Beats

To understand Bob in his later years was to understand the unique ecosystem of the North Berkeley poetry scene, a landscape distinct from its counterparts across the bay. While San Francisco’s North Beach was the epicenter of nocturnal jazz clubs, North Berkeley—particularly the “Gourmet Ghetto” corridor along Shattuck Avenue—became an intellectual open-air salon. Here, the boundaries between the university's academic elite, anti-war activists, and the literary underground completely dissolved. It was a fiercely democratic, collaborative community where a chapbook was handed off like a secret handshake, and poetry was treated as an essential, daily utility for survival. Even into his nineties, Bob remained the living center of that enduring literary landscape, holding forth daily at Peet's Coffee and Tea, in North Berkeley. Amidst the fresh ground of coffee, constant line of patient patrons, the steam of espresso machines - Bob held court with anyone willing to listen or argue. He was among the last living links to that foundational generation of Bay Area poets who believed that art belonged to the streets. In a region defined by institutional upheaval, Bob’s life and work never wavered from its core conviction: that poetry and political struggle are not separate arts, but of the very same breath.
