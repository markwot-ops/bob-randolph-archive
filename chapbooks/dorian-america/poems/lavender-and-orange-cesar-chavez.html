Lavender and orange Cesar Chavez

Cesar Chavez is gone, but he was once just like the little boy playing with two friends at the side of the dirt road. His roots are also in an older child, hungry and weary after picking grapes in the hot sun, his torn hat giving him little shelter from the San Joaquin sun. Cesar was one of the children in the Chavez family, with sisters and brothers. The Chavez family was like so many families, their origins in the Spanish language south of the border in Mexico, up here in California that once was Mexico, but was taken from them into the English-speaking world. Cesar is in every family, every Spanish-speaking hut, every Spanish-speaking group of Latinos talking softly in the shade of a tree somewhere between Fresno and Bakersfield. He will be in the laughter at weddings, the dancing at parties on Friday and Saturday nights, the sharing of food at a picnic table on Sunday afternoons. He will be present in steamy kitchens in small houses without air conditioning, the kisses at parting time between lovers, grandparents and grandchildren, both this year and on this same day a hundred years from now. He will be in the blood of his people, during their travail in a foreign land, a land that has become foreign to itself as well. He will be by the bedside with midwives, bringing new little chiquitos and chiquitas into the world. He will be there more and more in our Anglo world, as over the centuries to come, across the races and ethnic origins that so far keep us apart unless we struggle against it. Cesar Chavez will be one of the Caesars in the history books, one that Shakespeare did not write about, but may yet one day in other worlds. Cesar Chavez is an old redwood giant in our forest, a great expanse of dry land leading up to the wild hills to the southwest, he will be the lightning in gray skies, the downpour of rain in April afternoons, when the world is between hot and cold, between day and night, between life and death, as it has always been. He will be in all growing things, in the new spring grass that paints the wild areas green toward summer, in their dry parched brownness in August's heat. He will be in bird calls, in mocking birds, blue jays, crows and magpies, all living as fully as they can in their part of the world without the complexities of human life, of human survival. He will feel our lives, like his own, as he did when he lived among us. his own inadequacy, just as we feel ours, he is ultimately so like us, his hopes and labors for a better world, sometimes so lonely and tired late at night, the night before the beginning of another La Huelga pilgrimage, with one last cup of coffee before he turns out the lights and locks the door. The next 3 weeks to make history, to write a new chapter of California history between Delano and Sacramento, that the press cannot ignore no matter how it tries. Then, for the days and years to come, with the passage of the seasons we will think of him when the lavender lupines reappear like delicate blankets for a whole people. We will also think of him as the bright California poppies appear scattered in the heat of dry slopes, telling us again the forces of life are absolutely indestructible. These lavender lupines, these orange poppies, the colors of Cesar Chavez - we will keep in our memories as the seasons pass, our lavender and orange, our lavender and orange Cesar Chavez. Cesar - in his own immortal colors, so like a latter-day Cuatemoc in the gorgeous Aztec robes of long ago.
